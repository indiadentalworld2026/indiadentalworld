01

Executive summary

Six findings that should shape our product and our pitch.

  1. The category leader is not a dental productPracto Ray leads on brand and distribution, but it is a general clinic system. Its dental charting is widely described as generic, with no tooth-level history, weak lab-work tracking and no dental inventory module. The largest player is beatable on clinical depth.
  2. Our real competitor is paperThe dominant record system in Indian dental practice is a register, a WhatsApp thread and an Excel sheet. Every paid product in this market is fighting for a minority of clinics. That is an opportunity, not a warning.
  3. Dentists buy leads, not softwareThe strongest published reason to choose Practo Ray is access to its patient marketplace. Software is what the lead generation is wrapped in. This is exactly our model — but it also means we inherit Practo's central vulnerability: when leads slow, the subscription is cancelled.
  4. Record completeness is the industry's dirty secretMost clinics that "use software" use it for appointments and billing. Clinical detail — charting, notes, radiographs, consent — is thin or absent. Without it, none of the AI, analytics or interoperability value we plan can exist.
  5. "ABDM-ready" is mostly marketingABDM compliance is a formal, sequential certification through the National Health Authority sandbox, and vendors claiming readiness without completed milestones is a recognised problem in the market. Verified certification is a defensible differentiator very few can claim.
  6. The compliance clock is runningThe DPDP Rules were notified on 13 November 2025; substantive obligations become fully enforceable around 13 May 2027, with consent-manager provisions landing around November 2026. We have roughly eight months to build what most incumbents have not started.
02

A warning about the evidence

Read this before quoting any number in this document

Almost every "best dental software in India" comparison available online is published by a software vendor ranking its own competitors. Their figures for the same product disagree wildly — one source lists Practo Ray from roughly ₹12,000 per year, another quotes ₹5,000–15,000 per month, a third alleges commissions of up to 15% on marketplace bookings. These cannot all be true.

There is no independent, audited market-share data for Indian dental practice management software. Treat every share, price and adoption figure below as directional, not factual. Where we need defensible numbers for a fundraise or a board decision, they must come from our own primary research, not from competitor blogs.

Primary research we should commission before Series A

  • Structured interviews with 150–200 clinics across three cities, stratified by chairs and city tier.
  • Consented audit of record completeness in 30 clinics already using software — the single most valuable dataset nobody in this market has.
  • Mystery-shopper pricing exercise across the top eight vendors to establish real, negotiated price points.
  • Churn interviews with 25 clinics that cancelled a PMS subscription in the last 18 months.

Global and open-source

Dentrix / Dentrix Ascend, Open Dental, DentiMax, tab32, CureMD Dental.

Threat to us: the largest single competitor by clinic count. Most of our sales effort will be spent here.

What is actually widely used, and why

In order of estimated clinic count, with the caveat that no audited data exists:

1. No dedicated software

The clear majority of India's dental clinics, heavily weighted to solo practitioners and tier-2/3 cities. Register, diary, WhatsApp, Excel.

2. Practo Ray

The best-known paid option, chosen primarily for marketplace visibility rather than clinical capability. Strongest in metros.

3. Bestosys and dental-specialist PMS

Chosen by growth-minded owners and multi-chair clinics who have outgrown paper and want recall, CRM and real charting.

4. Global and open-source systems

A small tail — typically NRI-returned dentists, premium chains and academic institutions.

Why the leader leads

Not product quality. Practo leads on brand, distribution and the patient marketplace. An Indian dentist buys visibility first and gets software as a by-product. Every competitor that has tried to win on features alone has stayed small, because features do not fill an empty appointment book.

The lesson for our strategy

This validates our two-sided model and disqualifies a pure-SaaS approach. It also warns us: if demand is the reason clinics buy, demand is the reason they leave. Our retention must eventually come from operational lock-in — records, lab pipeline, inventory, payments — not from leads alone. Practo's weakness is that it never built that depth. Ours must be that we did.

07

The Practo question: software, or lead generator?

The honest answer: overwhelmingly a lead generator, with software as the wrapper and the lock-in mechanism.

The evidence

  • Third-party buyer guides consistently name marketplace discovery as Ray's single most compelling reason to buy, and generic dental charting as its main weakness. Software is not the purchase driver.
  • Discoverability in patient search generally requires a paid listing on top of the software subscription — a marketing spend that only makes sense if discovery is the growth engine.
  • Alternative vendors market directly against per-booking commission and listing fees, positioning "own your patient relationships" as the switching argument — which only works if dentists feel they are renting patients.
  • The structural complaint from established practices is lead leakage: a patient searching for their own dentist is shown competitors nearby. That is a marketplace dynamic, not a software one.
  • Dentist-side commentary going back a decade has attacked the per-lead economics as poor value against generic local directories, and consumer review platforms carry heavy complaint volume about listings, refunds and profile control.

What this means for us — three consequences

  1. Leads are the sale; software is the moatSell visibility, because that is what opens the wallet. Then make the software so operationally embedded that cancelling becomes impossible even in a slow lead month.
  2. Never charge commission per bookingPer-booking pricing punishes the clinic for growing and turns us into an adversary. Flat, transparent pricing — and we monetise the ecosystem instead. This is a direct, marketable attack on the leader's pricing model.
  3. Solve lead leakage explicitlyA clinic's own returning patients must never be shown competitors. Distinguish new-patient discovery from existing-patient booking and guarantee it in writing. This single policy is a sales weapon against Practo.
08

Do dentists actually update clinical records?

Mostly, no — and this is the most important operational fact in this entire document, because every AI, analytics and interoperability ambition we have depends on data that is currently not being entered.

This section is structural reasoning, not measured data

No published study reliably quantifies clinical record completeness in Indian dental practice. What follows is a model built from observable market signals — including the documented reality that many dentists remain unaware even of mandatory AERB radiography registration, which suggests administrative and record-keeping discipline is generally low. Validating this model with primary research is the highest-value study we can run, and would itself be a publishable asset that establishes us as the authority in this market.

The usage pyramid

Level 1 — Billing only

Software used to generate invoices and track payments because money forces discipline. Clinical fields empty.

Level 2 — Billing plus appointments

Calendar and reminders added. Patient names and phone numbers accurate; clinical data still minimal.

Level 3 — Partial clinical

Free-text notes, sometimes a chart, occasional radiograph upload. Inconsistent between doctors in the same clinic. Unusable for analytics.

Level 4 — Complete structured records

Charting, coded diagnoses, plans, consent, images, prescriptions, outcomes. A small minority — typically multi-chair clinics with a practice manager, or owners preparing to sell or scale.

Why records do not get updated — and what each cause demands from our product

CauseProduct response required
Typing during treatment is impossible — gloved hands, patient in the chairVoice-first capture; the dentist speaks, the record writes itself. Non-negotiable.
No perceived return — records feel like paperwork for someone else's benefitEvery entry must visibly produce something the dentist wants: a recall, a payment, an insight. Section 9.
Software is slower than the paper it replacedThree taps or fewer for every common action; benchmark against a paper file, not against competitors.
Nobody enforces it — no audit, no insurer, no consequenceMake our own value conditional on completeness; give the clinic its own internal completeness score.

Making the case to dentists: what complete records are actually worth

Dentists do not respond to "compliance" or "best practice." They respond to money, risk and time. Here are seven arguments, ordered by persuasive power in a real sales conversation.

Argument 1

Recall revenue you are currently throwing away

A complete record is the only way to know that a patient is due for a scaling, a second-phase treatment, or a crown check. Clinics without records cannot recall; clinics with them generate appointments from patients they already have, at effectively zero acquisition cost.

Demo: show the clinic's own incomplete-treatment list on day one of onboarding.

Argument 2

Treatment acceptance goes up when the patient can see the plan

Most diagnosed treatment in a typical practice is never completed. A recorded, visualised, costed plan the patient can take home converts markedly better than a verbal explanation and a scribble.

Demo: acceptance rate by doctor and by procedure, visible in the dashboard.

Argument 3

Medico-legal defence

In a consumer complaint or negligence claim, the contemporaneous record is the defence. An audit-trailed digital record with timestamped consent is far stronger evidence than a register entry that can be alleged to have been written later.

Demo: show the audit trail and consent artefact for a sample case.

Argument 4

Your practice is worth more with records

A practice with a complete, transferable patient database and documented revenue history sells for a materially higher multiple than one whose value walks out with the owner. Records convert a job into an asset.

Demo: a practice valuation summary generated from their own data.

Argument 5

Associates and multi-chair accountability

The moment a second dentist joins, the owner needs to know what was done, by whom, at what price, with what outcome. Records are the only way to run a practice you are not personally standing in.

Demo: doctor-wise production, collection and rework reporting.

Argument 6

Insurance, claims and corporate tie-ups

Corporate panels, insurers and government schemes require structured documentation. Clinics without it are excluded from revenue streams that are growing as digital claims infrastructure matures.

Demo: a claim-ready encounter bundle produced automatically.

Argument 7

AI only works if you have fed it

Every AI benefit we offer — risk prediction, no-show forecasting, treatment suggestions, benchmarking — requires the clinic's own history. The dentist who records today gets the intelligence tomorrow; the one who doesn't is permanently locked out.

Demo: benchmarks unlocked as completeness score rises.

The design principle behind all seven

Never ask a dentist to maintain records. Ask them to do something they already want — bill the patient, send the recall, get the lab case back, close the treatment plan — and capture the record as the by-product. Documentation as a goal fails everywhere in the world. Documentation as a side effect succeeds.

How many existing products are fully compliant?

On the available evidence: very few, and possibly none across the full stack.

What we can say with confidence

  • ABDM claims are routinely inflated. Claiming to be "ABDM-ready" without completing any milestone certification with the National Health Authority is documented as a common market practice. The simple test a buyer should apply is whether the vendor can produce milestone certification — and most cannot.
  • A handful have real ABDM standing. Practo Ray is described as having a mature ABDM position, and products such as MocDoc and Eka Care are positioned as ABDM-aligned. Others are described as only partially integrated.
  • Common failure modes are specific and technical. Treating ABHA as an ID field without the full consent and care-context flow; partial FHIR mapping where one bundle type works and others are broken or missing; holding patient data under vendor-managed encryption keys when key custody should remain with the healthcare entity; and failing to retain consent artefacts as evidence for an auditor or a court.
  • DPDP readiness is largely unbuilt across the market. With substantive obligations not fully enforceable until around May 2027, most vendors have not yet implemented granular consent, erasure, breach workflows or grievance mechanisms.
  • EHR Standards 2016 adoption appears minimal. Structured SNOMED CT coding is rare in Indian dental software; free-text notes dominate. This is the deepest and least contested gap in the market.

The strategic read

Compliance is usually a cost centre. Here it is a land grab. If we complete ABDM M1–M3 with certification, build DPDP obligations into the product before the deadline, and code clinical data to SNOMED CT from the first record, we will be able to make a claim no competitor can match — and one that serious clinics, chains, corporate panels and insurers actively screen for. It also raises the floor for the dozen sub-scale dental PMS vendors who cannot afford this work, which accelerates consolidation in our favour.

Scoring and remediation sequence

How to score

Each of the 74 checks is scored Pass (2), Partial (1) or Fail (0), giving a maximum of 148. Report the score by domain, not just in total — a strong average hiding a failed ABDM or consent domain is a false reassurance. Any Fail in Domain B, C, E or F is a release blocker regardless of total score.

PriorityDomainsRationale
NowF (security), E (audit & retention), B (consent)These are unfixable retrospectively. Records created without an audit trail or a consent artefact can never be made compliant later — they have to be re-collected. Every day we operate without them creates permanent liability.
NowD (clinical standards)Same logic. Free-text notes captured today cannot be retro-coded to SNOMED CT reliably. Coding from the first record is the difference between a data asset and a data swamp.
Next 6 monthsC (ABDM), G (breach & governance)ABDM certification runs five to ten months end to end including the safe-to-host audit, so it must start now to land before the DPDP deadline. Governance must be in place before we hold data at scale.
Next 6 monthsI (marketplace & advertising)Advertising rules for dental practitioners are the single most under-appreciated risk in our model and must be resolved before the marketplace scales.
OngoingA (access), H (clinical & AI)Iterative; tighten as the product and the AI feature set grow.

  4. Book the CERT-In empanelled security auditScheduling lead times are long and the certificate must name the exact environment tested.
